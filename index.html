TF‑IDF at a glance

TF‑IDF(t, d) = TF(t, d) × IDF(t) — a word scores high when it appears often in one document but rarely across the rest of the corpus, which is exactly the kind of word that best describes what that document is about.

🤖 Language Models

Models that learn the underlying structure of language itself, enabling prediction, generation, and deep contextual understanding.

No language model concepts match your search/filter.

Evolution of Language Models

Statistical Language Models

Probability-based, count-driven models (e.g. N-grams) that estimate word sequences from corpus frequency.

Neural Language Models

RNN/LSTM-based models that learn dense representations and carry context across a sequence.

Transformer-based Representations

Self-attention models that process full sequences in parallel, powering today's state-of-the-art NLP systems.

Section B

⚖️ Comparative Analysis

Five side-by-side comparisons of related techniques covered in this repository — mechanism, complexity, strengths, weaknesses, and best-fit use cases.

1. Stemming vs. Lemmatization

StemmingLemmatization
MechanismRule-based suffix strippingDictionary + grammar-based lookup
ComplexityLow — fast, rule-basedModerate — needs POS tagging & lexicon
StrengthsVery fast, simple to implementProduces real, accurate words
WeaknessesCan produce non-wordsSlower, resource-heavy
Best forLarge-scale search/indexingTasks needing accurate meaning (chatbots, QA)

2. TF vs. TF‑IDF

TFTF‑IDF
MechanismFrequency within a single documentFrequency weighted by corpus-wide rarity
ComplexityLowLow–moderate (needs full corpus stats)
StrengthsSimple, fast to computeDown-weights generic/common words
WeaknessesCommon words dominateStill ignores word order/semantics
Best forQuick keyword frequency checksSearch ranking, document keyword extraction

3. Word2Vec vs. FastText

Word2VecFastText
MechanismPredicts word from context (CBOW/Skip-gram)Same, but on character n-grams (sub-words)
ComplexityModerateModerate–high (more parameters)
StrengthsFast, strong semantic analogiesHandles OOV & rare/morphological words
WeaknessesFails on unseen/out-of-vocabulary wordsLarger model size
Best forEnglish/well-resourced languagesMorphologically rich, regional languages

4. Statistical vs. Neural Language Models

Statistical LMsNeural LMs
MechanismFrequency-based N-gram probabilitiesLearned dense representations (RNN/LSTM)
ComplexityLowHigh — needs GPU training, large data
StrengthsSimple, interpretable, fastCaptures longer context, generalizes better
WeaknessesSparse for large N, no real contextSequential training is slow
Best forLightweight, low-resource setupsText generation, translation, sequence tasks

5. One-Hot Encoding vs. Word Embeddings

One-Hot EncodingWord Embeddings
MechanismBinary sparse vector, one index per wordDense vector learned from context
ComplexityVery low, but memory-heavy at scaleModerate — requires training/pre-trained models
StrengthsSimple, no training neededCaptures semantic similarity
WeaknessesNo notion of word similarity, huge dimensionalityNeeds large corpora to train well
Best forSmall, categorical vocabulariesMost modern NLP tasks

🔀 Workflow Diagrams

Hover or tap any step below to see a short explanation of what happens at that stage.

Workflow 1 — Complete Text Preprocessing Pipeline

Raw text is progressively cleaned and normalized until it's ready for feature extraction.

Raw Text
Unprocessed input text exactly as collected — may contain noise, casing, and punctuation.
→
Sentence Segmentation
Splits the text block into individual sentences for sentence-level processing.
→
Tokenization
Breaks each sentence into individual word/punctuation tokens.
→
Case Normalization
Converts all tokens to a consistent case, usually lowercase.
→
Stop-word Removal
Removes common low-information words like "the", "is", "a".
→
Noise / Text Cleaning
Strips URLs, HTML tags, special characters, and fixes formatting issues.
→
Stemming / Lemmatization
Reduces words to their root or dictionary base form.
→
Processed Text
Clean, structured text — ready for feature engineering.

Workflow 2 — Feature Engineering Pipeline

Processed text is converted into numerical features that reflect word importance.

Processed Text
Clean, tokenized text output from Workflow 1.
→
Bag of Words / N-Grams
Counts word (or word-sequence) occurrences to build a vocabulary-based vector.
→
TF
Measures how frequently each term appears within a document.
→
IDF
Measures how rare each term is across the whole corpus.
→
TF‑IDF
Combines TF and IDF into a single importance score per term.
→
Feature Vector
The final numeric vector representing the document, ready for a model.

Workflow 3 — Text-to-Vector Transformation

The general path from raw language to a machine-learning-ready numerical vector.

Raw Text
The original, unprocessed sentence or document.
→
Tokenization
Splits text into discrete word/sub-word tokens.
→
Representation Technique
One-Hot, Word2Vec, FastText, or Contextual Embeddings applied to tokens.
→
Numerical Vector
Dense or sparse vector encoding the token/document.
→
NLP / ML Model
The vector is fed into a classifier, translator, or language model.

Section E

🔬 Research & Industry Insights

Two research developments, two industrial applications, and one open-source framework that connect directly to the concepts in this repository. Sources are listed in the References section.

Research

The Transformer Architecture

The 2017 paper "Attention Is All You Need" introduced self-attention, removing the need for recurrence and enabling the parallel training of much larger language models. It underlies today's Transformer-based representations.

Research

Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG)

Introduced in 2020, RAG combines a language model with a document retriever, letting the model "look up" relevant text before generating a response — directly connecting search/TF‑IDF-style retrieval with modern language models.

Industry

Search Engines using BERT

Google announced in 2019 that it had incorporated BERT, a Transformer-based contextual embedding model, into Search to better understand the intent behind longer, conversational queries.

Industry

AI Customer Support Assistants

Enterprises widely deploy chatbot and virtual-assistant systems built on contextual embeddings and Transformer models to automate customer support, using the same NLP pipeline covered in this repository.

Open Source

Hugging Face Transformers

An open-source library providing pre-trained Transformer models (e.g. BERT, GPT-family) and tokenizers, widely used in both academia and industry to build NLP applications without training models from scratch.

Citizen Services

Natural Language Processing has a direct role to play in making information, services, and opportunities more accessible, which connects it closely to several sustainable development goals such as quality education, reduced inequality, and inclusive institutions.

Language Accessibility. Techniques like tokenization, machine translation, and contextual embeddings allow content originally written in one language to reach speakers of another. This matters in a linguistically diverse country like India, where a large share of the population is more comfortable in a regional language than in English. Translation and summarization tools built on the concepts in this repository can make government notices, news, or educational material available to far more people than before.

Regional Language Technologies. Models such as FastText, which represent words using sub-word character n-grams, are particularly well suited to morphologically rich Indian languages like Hindi, Marathi, or Tamil, where a single root word can take many inflected forms. Investing in NLP for regional languages helps prevent a "digital divide" where only speakers of a handful of global languages benefit from AI-powered tools.

Educational Inclusion. NLP-powered tools — automatic summarizers, question-answering systems, and grammar/spell checkers built on lemmatization and language models — can help students learn at their own pace, get instant feedback on writing, and access simplified versions of complex material. This is especially valuable for first-generation learners or students studying in a second language.

Assistive Technologies. Text-to-speech and speech-to-text systems, which rely on language models and tokenization internally, help visually impaired or hearing-impaired users interact with digital content. Predictive text and autocomplete, powered by statistical and neural language models, also reduce the effort needed for users with motor disabilities to communicate.

Citizen Services. Government and public-service chatbots that use intent understanding and contextual embeddings can answer citizen queries about schemes, documents, or procedures in plain language, at any hour, reducing the friction of navigating bureaucratic processes — especially useful for citizens with limited digital literacy.

Section G

💭 My Learning Reflection

Building this repository helped us understand NLP as a pipeline rather than a single technique. We started by learning why raw text can't be fed directly into a model — preprocessing steps like tokenization, stop-word removal, and lemmatization were needed just to get text into a usable, consistent shape. It was interesting to see how something as simple-sounding as "stemming a word" actually involves trade-offs between speed and accuracy.

Feature engineering was where things started connecting to math we'd only seen abstractly before — actually computing TF and IDF by hand for a small example made the formula click in a way that just reading about it never did. Moving into representation, comparing One-Hot Encoding, Word2Vec, and FastText side by side made the progression obvious: each technique exists specifically to fix a limitation of the one before it, ending with contextual embeddings solving the "same word, different meaning" problem that static embeddings couldn't.

The comparison tables were the most challenging part to write well — it's easy to say two techniques are "different," but explaining computational complexity and genuinely different use-cases required us to actually understand the internals, not just repeat definitions. Researching real applications and current industry use (like BERT in search) also made the concepts feel less academic and more like things we'll actually encounter working with text data in the future.

Overall, this activity gave us a working mental model of the NLP pipeline — from raw text to a model-ready vector — that we expect to build on directly in the rest of this course, especially once we move from these foundational techniques into full language models and deep learning-based NLP.

Reference

📖 Glossary

Quick definitions for terms used throughout this repository — searchable.

Token
The smallest unit of text after tokenization — typically a word or punctuation mark.
Corpus
A large, structured collection of text used to train or evaluate NLP models.
Vocabulary
The complete set of unique tokens/words known to a model or dataset.
Stop Word
A very common word (e.g. "the", "is") often removed for carrying little unique meaning.
Stemming
Rule-based reduction of a word to its root form by stripping suffixes.
Lemmatization
Dictionary and grammar-based reduction of a word to its true base form.
Vector
A list of numbers representing a word, sentence, or document numerically.
Embedding
A dense vector representation where semantically similar items are positioned close together.
N-Gram
A contiguous sequence of N words or characters extracted from text.
TF
Term Frequency — how often a term appears within a single document.
IDF
Inverse Document Frequency — how rare a term is across an entire corpus.
TF-IDF
A combined score of TF and IDF used to weigh word importance in a document.
Language Model
A model that estimates the probability of word sequences in a language.
Transformer
A neural network architecture using self-attention to process sequences in parallel.
